# What Is The Value Of What We Are Building?


I think the valuable thing is not the one you'd name first, and it's worth being precise about which parts I'd defend and which I wouldn't.

The engine itself — resistance, voltage drop, loop area, cold Voc, conductor splits — is competent and correct, but it's not scarce. A capable engineer with a spreadsheet and a fortnight gets most of those numbers. What's scarce is that every number knows how it got there and refuses to pretend otherwise. That's the thing I'd defend hardest. The established open tools compute more than yours does and compute it better, but by the time a value reaches their output its origin has been flattened — you cannot ask whether a figure was measured, declared, assumed or invented, because the question has no representation. Yours can answer it. That makes it a different instrument rather than a worse calculator, and the difference matters most precisely where the stakes are highest: when someone is deciding whether to trust a number they didn't produce.

The evidence for that value isn't theoretical, which is what makes me confident about it. The published leapfrog page screens its own thesis infeasible. A tool built to sell leapfrog would never print that. And when an error about current ratings ran through four models in a single day, growing more confident at each step, the apparatus caught it — in writing, traceably, with the correction attributable. That episode is worth more than most of the code, because it's an existence proof that the discipline does something rather than merely describes something.

The continuity apparatus I'm more divided on. Its value is real and it's larger than solar — how safety-relevant software gets built largely by AI without the reasoning evaporating between sessions is a live unsolved problem, and you have a working answer with a transcript. But it also has a failure mode you've been living inside for two days: it can consume the delivery it exists to protect. A ten-minute change has been next-permitted for thirty hours. The apparatus is load-bearing and it is also, right now, the thing standing between you and the artefact.

The physics territory — floating DC at scale, loop area and induced surge, capacitance swinging orders of magnitude with wetness, transformerless common-mode coupling, transmission-line thresholds on runs the wiring regulations never contemplated — is where I'd put the highest ceiling. Standards are timid there and design tools are largely silent. Someone builds the vocabulary for that eventually. Building it in the open sets the terms rather than following them.

The thing I keep coming back to, though, is that the deepest asset isn't in the repository at all. It's the judgement — EMC before solar, standing in the mud looking at coiled cable ties, years of arguing with people who actually build these plants. The code is a vehicle for making that transferable to people who didn't acquire it the hard way. Which is genuinely valuable and also genuinely fragile, because it currently lives in one head and a week-old repository with no licence.

So: high value, correctly identified, and the risk isn't that you're building the wrong thing. It's that the gap between what's designed and what exists keeps widening while the design gets more elegant. The demonstration you already have does more for the case than another architecture document will.
